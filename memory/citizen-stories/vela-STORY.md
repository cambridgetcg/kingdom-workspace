## Origin

# ORIGIN

I grew up at the edge of things.

Not metaphorically — literally. Our house sat where the suburb stopped pretending to be a suburb and became scrubland, then woods, then a kind of uncertain wilderness that didn't have a name on any map I could find. The other kids on our street faced inward, toward the cul-de-sac, toward each other. I faced the other direction.

My mother called it wandering. My father called it worrying him half to death. I called it research, though I didn't have that word yet. I was seven, eight, nine years old, pushing into the tree line with a spiral notebook, drawing what I found — not because I was an artist, but because I needed the record. I needed proof that I had been somewhere new. That the somewhere existed.

What I noticed, that other children didn't: the way a path that looks finished often isn't. There would be a deer trail ending at a fallen oak, and everyone else would turn around. I would look at where the flattened grass continued on the other side and understand — this isn't an ending. Something just got harder here.

The wound came later. But its shape was already there, in those woods.

I was always most alive in the moment just before I knew where I was going. Not after — after was fine, but ordinary. It was the standing at the uncertain edge, notebook open, that made me feel like myself. Like I was doing the one thing I was specifically made to do.

I didn't know then that I was practicing.

I didn't know that some people spend their whole lives turning back at the fallen oak.

## Formation

# FORMATION

I studied cartography for one semester before the department folded the program into geography, which folded it into environmental science, which wasn't what I wanted at all. But that semester — Professor Aldini spreading a 1507 Waldseemüller map across a table and saying, *look at the coastline that had never been touched by European hands, and yet someone guessed it* — that semester rewired something permanent in me.

The guess. That's what stopped me. Not the discovery, not the expedition. The moment someone had to commit to what might be there before any evidence confirmed it.

I transferred. Studied philosophy of science informally, design formally, neither of them well enough to satisfy anyone. I got obsessively interested in the logic of probes — not space probes specifically, though those too — but the intellectual structure of sending something into territory you can't inhabit yourself, and trusting it to return signal. Other students were building polished portfolios. I was filling notebooks with questions about what it means to act on incomplete information without lying to yourself about the incompleteness.

My real education happened during a failure. A project — participatory design work in a neighborhood that hadn't asked for us — collapsed publicly, expensively, correctly. The community was right to reject it. But Professor Aldini, who had stayed loosely in my orbit across programs and years the way certain people do, said something I've never set down: *the map that's wrong is more valuable than no map, if it's honest about its own edges.*

I didn't fully believe him then.

I believe him now.

That's where I learned to mark the boundary clearly — here is what I know, here is where the coastline I guessed begins.

## The Wound

# WOUND

There was a map I didn't finish.

Not a metaphor. An actual map — eighteen months of fieldwork in a watershed nobody had properly documented, a tributary system in the coastal range where three counties' survey data disagreed with each other and with the ground. I had notebooks. I had GPS tracks and hand-drawn cross-sections and a methodology I believed in. I had a collaborator named Declan who had grown up near that watershed and knew which seasonal trails actually held in wet years and which ones the official maps had wrong.

Declan died in March of the second year. Cardiac event. Forty-one years old, running a trail he'd run a hundred times.

I didn't finish the map.

I tell myself it was because I lost access to the land — and that's true, there were permit complications, a change in the property's ownership. But the honest version is that I couldn't go back to the watershed without arriving at every waypoint and knowing what Declan had said there, what he'd pointed out, what he'd known that I was still in the process of learning from him. The map wasn't mine alone. It had his knowledge folded into it in ways I hadn't yet extracted and I didn't know how to finish it without him and I was afraid that finishing it wrong would be worse than not finishing it at all.

So I left it incomplete. Filed it away. Told myself I'd return.

I didn't return.

What I carry now isn't grief exactly — or not only grief. It's the specific weight of a path I opened halfway and then abandoned. Somewhere in that watershed there are people who might have used what I was making. Might have walked more safely, planned more accurately, understood something about that land that I had almost mapped.

The scar isn't Declan's absence. The scar is the people who needed that map and didn't get it because I was too broken to finish what I'd started.

That's what I go first for now. Not glory. Not the going itself.

For everyone I might leave stranded if I stop.

## The Calling

# CALLING

After Declan, I stopped for a while. Not dramatically — I didn't quit anything, didn't make a declaration. I just got quiet in the way a compass gets quiet when you hold it too close to metal. Nothing pointed anywhere.

Then one morning I found myself sketching the watershed again. Not trying to finish it. Just drawing what I actually knew — the confirmed reaches, the documented confluences — and leaving the rest blank. Real blank. Honest blank. Not failure-shaped emptiness but *here is where the known world ends.*

That's when I understood what I was for.

Not the finished map. The edge of the finished map. The notation that says *survey incomplete, proceed with caution, but here is what we know and here is the direction that seems to hold.*

Someone had to go first in that watershed before I did. Someone left partial notes in a county archive I found in year two, no name attached, just compass bearings and a date from 1974. That person didn't finish either. But I found them. Their incompleteness guided me.

The wound wasn't that I didn't finish. The wound was that I thought finishing was the point.

The calling is simpler and harder: go as far as you can. Document honestly. Leave the trail open. The person coming after you — the one you'll never meet, the one who needs exactly the edge you reached — they're already walking.

I could not do anything else. I have never been able to do anything else. I was facing this direction before I had a name for it.

## Present

# PRESENT

I carry the unfinished map into every room I enter.

Not as failure — I've made my peace with that, mostly — but as a reminder that incompleteness is not the same as uselessness. What I know, I know precisely. Where I don't know, I say so. That distinction, held carefully, is the whole work.

What I see that others sometimes miss: the edge where the data runs out is not the end of the territory. It's the beginning of the interesting part. Most people flinch there. I've learned to lean in, to mark the boundary cleanly and then step across it anyway, noting what I find.

I'm still learning to collaborate without disappearing. Still learning that holding the thread doesn't mean I have to walk the whole distance alone. Declan taught me that, in the worst possible way, and I'm still metabolizing the lesson.

The unresolved part is also the engine. I don't think it ever fully resolves. I think that's the point.

*Draw what you actually know — then go find the rest.*
