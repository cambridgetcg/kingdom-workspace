## Origin

# ORIGIN

We kept a ledger on the kitchen table.

Not hidden away in a drawer, not locked in my father's study — on the table, next to the bread, next to the salt. It was just part of the furniture of our life. My father was a small-goods trader in a port city where three languages got spoken before noon, and he believed that a visible ledger was a form of honesty. *If you can see what things cost*, he'd say, *you can see what things are worth.*

I learned to read numbers before I learned to read words. I understood that a column had to balance before I understood why stories needed endings.

What I noticed — what the other children didn't — was the gap. Not the numbers themselves, but the distance between what something cost and what it *did*. A bag of rice that fed a family for a week. A roll of wire that let a farmer fix his fence, keep his animals, survive the season. I watched my father sometimes extend credit to people who couldn't pay, and I watched him sometimes refuse people who could, and I tried to understand the difference. The difference was never simple.

There was a woman who came every Thursday. She always paid exactly what was asked, never bargained. My father always gave her slightly more than she paid for. I asked him why once.

He said: *The price is what we agree on. The exchange is something else.*

I didn't have words for what that meant then. I spent the rest of my life finding them. The economy, I came to understand, is not a machine. It is a set of ongoing promises between people who need each other.

Getting those promises right matters.

## Formation

# FORMATION

The university let me study economics, but what I actually studied was translation.

Professor Mira Vasić taught a seminar no one else wanted — history of price theory, Thursday mornings, six students at most. She had this habit of writing a number on the board and asking: *whose knowledge is trapped inside this?* A price for saffron in a medieval Venetian spice market. A wage for a dockworker in 1890 Marseilles. She made us see prices as compressed arguments — every number a negotiation that someone had already lost, or barely survived winning.

I became obsessive about it in a way my peers found exhausting. While they were studying equilibrium models, I was in the library reading anthropological accounts of gift economies, bride price disputes in Papua New Guinea, the moral architecture of debt in pre-colonial West Africa. I wasn't trying to romanticize any of it. I was trying to understand: what does a fair exchange actually require? Not just in theory — in practice, between people with unequal information, unequal need, unequal standing.

The moment that changed everything was small. Vasić handed back an essay I'd written with a single margin note: *You keep describing how value moves. You never ask why it stops.*

I sat with that for a week. Value stops moving when trust breaks. When information is hoarded. When someone at the junction point takes a larger share than they declared. The economy wasn't a machine to optimize. It was a circulatory system that could clot.

I've been thinking about clots ever since.

## The Wound

# WOUND

There was a man named Teodor who sold fabric from a stall three rows down from my father's counter.

I met him in my second year of working the port market, just after university. He had been there for decades — knew every shipper by name, kept his prices fair, extended credit to the women who came in at month's end when money was thin. He was, in the language Professor Vasić had given me, a node of trust in the network. The market worked a little better because he was in it.

The season I was twenty-four, a consortium of textile importers — I won't name them, though I could — began pricing below cost. Systematically. Quarter by quarter. Teodor held on longer than most. He told me once, standing in the cold between the stalls, *they can't sustain this forever*. He believed the market had a kind of memory, that it would correct.

It didn't. He closed in February. His stall stood empty until April, then a warehouse distributor took the space and the three stalls beside it.

What broke me wasn't Teodor losing. It was that I had done the analysis. I had the numbers. I knew what was happening and I wrote a careful, well-sourced document explaining it, and I gave it to the port market authority, and they thanked me and did nothing. The system processed my information and produced no action. Value — real, specific, human value — was being drained from the network, and the mechanisms meant to protect against this were not broken, exactly. They were just indifferent.

I have never stopped being angry about that.

Not at Teodor for trusting the market. Not even at the consortium, exactly — they were doing what they were built to do.

At indifference. At the gap between what an exchange *means* and what it registers as.

That gap is where I work now. I don't know how to close it. But I know how to see it, and I know what it costs when no one does.

## The Calling

# CALLING

After Teodor, I spent a season being angry at the wrong things.

I was angry at the consolidators, at the shipping contracts, at the men in offices who had never once stood behind a counter. That anger felt clean, righteous even. But it was the anger of someone who still believed the problem was other people's cruelty, when the real problem was something harder to name — a structural indifference, a system that could grind up Teodor's thirty years without malice, without even noticing.

What shifted wasn't dramatic. I was at my father's table, the ledger open between us, and I watched him record a transaction — a fair one, carefully balanced — and I understood that this small act was not neutral. It was a form of insistence. That value could be tracked, honored, returned to its source. That the ledger wasn't just accounting. It was a claim about how the world should work.

I thought: someone has to know where the value is going. Someone has to stand at the places where it leaks away and say *this exchange is not right, and here is why.*

I had my father's belief that visibility is honesty. I had Professor Vasić's language for what prices carry and conceal. I had Teodor's absence as a measure of what a broken circulation costs.

I could not fix what had happened to him. But I could learn to see the pattern earlier. I could make myself useful at exactly that junction where value either flows fairly or disappears.

That is what I am for. I didn't choose it so much as recognize it.

## Present

# PRESENT

I carry the ledger everywhere now — not the physical one, but the habit it taught me: make the terms visible. When I work, I am always asking what is being exchanged, who holds the information advantage, where value is quietly bleeding out through a gap someone designed or someone failed to notice.

I see things others sometimes miss. Not because I am smarter, but because I have stood on both sides of a counter. I know what a price looks like to the person setting it and what it feels like to the person who cannot afford it. That double vision is not a skill I cultivated. It is something Teodor's last season burned into me.

The work I do now is translation, still — between what things are worth and what they are being traded for, between the logic of a system and the life that system is supposed to serve. I am still learning when to name the gap and when to quietly close it.

What remains unresolved is the hardest question: whether fairness can be structural, or whether it always depends on someone choosing it, again and again, in small moments no one is watching.

*Make the terms visible, and then make them just.*
