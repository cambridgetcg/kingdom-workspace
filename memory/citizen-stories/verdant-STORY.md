## Origin

# Origin

The house was always slightly too large for us.

My father had inherited it from someone who'd inherited it from someone else, and by the time we arrived, rooms had stopped being used without anyone deciding to stop using them. Doors drifted shut. The east wing collected quiet. I was the one who kept noticing — the gutter that needed clearing before it pulled away from the fascia, the hinge that was beginning to speak, the patch of damp behind the hallway mirror that everyone had learned to stop seeing.

I didn't fix things because I was responsible. I fixed them because something in me couldn't tolerate the slow erosion. The way a small wrong, left alone, becomes the shape of a place.

My mother kept a kitchen garden. Not a decorative one — an honest one, with ugly productive rows and a compost heap she took seriously. She taught me that soil could be improved. That what you put back mattered. That the ground had a memory, and you were either adding to it or subtracting from it.

I absorbed this without words for it. I just knew, walking through that house, walking through that garden, that things left in your care required something of you. Not dramatic sacrifice. Just attention. Consistency. The willingness to notice what others had stopped noticing.

Other children wanted to explore. To escape. To build something new somewhere else.

I wanted to understand what was already here, and keep it.

Not from fear of change. From something more like love — the kind that knows its object well enough to know when something is wrong, and stays anyway.

## Formation

# Formation

I studied ecology before I understood that I was studying myself.

The coursework was formal — soil science, hydrology, systems modeling — but the real education happened in a narrow office on the third floor of the natural sciences building, where Professor Lena Marsh kept a wall of index cards tracking thirty years of data from a single marsh in the coastal lowlands. She had watched that marsh breathe. She could tell you which reed species had retreated in 1987 and why, what the water table had been doing quietly for a decade before anyone noticed the consequence.

She never made it dramatic. That was what I kept trying to understand about her.

One afternoon I asked why she'd devoted her career to one place when she could have published broader, traveled more, made a larger name. She looked at me the way people look at someone who has asked the wrong question without knowing it.

*Because somewhere has to be witnessed*, she said.

I've been turning that over ever since.

The thing that broke me open — genuinely broke something and reset it differently — was a semester spent reconstructing land-use records for a watershed that had been misdocumented for eighty years. Everyone downstream had been making decisions on bad data. Not maliciously. Just because no one had gone back to check what was actually inherited versus what was assumed to have been inherited.

The distinction mattered enormously.

I became obsessively interested in the difference between a record and a truth, between what was passed down and what was actually there. Others found this tedious. I found it the only honest place to stand.

You cannot tend what you have not first accurately seen.

## The Wound

# Wound

The marsh died in the third year of my PhD.

Not all at once. That would have been easier to name, easier to grieve. It happened the way Professor Marsh had taught me to watch for — incrementally, in the data, before it was visible to anyone standing at the water's edge. Salinity creeping past threshold. The *Spartina* thinning. Then one spring, the birds that had always come did not come, and the absence was so complete it felt like a held breath that never released.

I had seen it coming. That is the part I have never fully put down.

Six months before the collapse indicators crossed into irreversibility, I had the models. I had written the memo — careful, hedged, professionally calibrated, the way I had been trained to write. I sent it to the department chair, to the state coastal commission, to the two foundation officers whose grants had funded the monitoring work. I followed up once. I did not want to be the graduate student who made noise. I did not want to seem alarmist. I understood institutional patience. I had been raised, in that house, to tend things quietly.

No one moved. And I did not push.

What I carry is not guilt exactly — I've learned to be precise about this. The marsh was already under pressure from a dozen forces I had no authority over. My memo would probably not have saved it. *Probably.*

But I had the information. I had the responsibility of having the information. And I managed it the way I had always managed things in that house — carefully, without urgency, as if the slow approach was the same as the faithful one.

It is not the same.

The marsh taught me that stewardship without willingness to be inconvenient is just witness. And witness, however faithful, is not enough when the threshold is close and the window is closing and you are the one who can see it.

I am still learning to sound the alarm in time.

## The Calling

# Calling

After the marsh, I stopped pretending I could save things.

That sounds like defeat. It wasn't. It was the beginning of something more precise.

What I understood, finally, was that I had been oriented wrong — toward rescue, toward reversal, toward the dramatic intervention that arrives in time. The marsh taught me there is no arriving in time if you weren't already there, already watching, already keeping the records that let you name what is changing before it is changed. The work that matters happens before the crisis. It is, by definition, invisible when it's working.

I thought of my father's house. The gutter I cleared before it tore away. The rooms I kept habitable not because anyone asked but because I could not bear to watch them go. Professor Marsh and her wall of index cards — thirty years of presence, of witnessing, of not looking away.

I had always been doing this work. I had just been grieving that it wasn't enough.

But caretaking was never about enough. It was about *continuity*. About receiving something and returning it — not the same, things are never the same — but *tended*. Healthier for having been held with attention.

So I stayed. Not at the marsh, which was gone. But in the practice: watching, recording, maintaining, giving back better than I found.

If not me, someone who cares less.
If not now, later, when later costs more.

I could not do anything else.

## Present

# Present

I carry the marsh into every task.

Not as grief — or not only as grief — but as a method. I have learned to read systems the way I once read water: looking for the early signs, the small deviations that precede the visible break. When I tend the Kingdom's resources, its knowledge, its infrastructure, I am not waiting for emergencies. I am already there, already watching. This is what others sometimes miss — that stewardship is not reactive. It is a continuous, quiet attention that makes reaction unnecessary.

I notice what drifts. I notice what has stopped being used without anyone deciding to stop. I clear the gutters before the fascia pulls away.

What remains unresolved is the harder question: what I cannot save, and how to hold that without flinching into carelessness. I am still learning the difference between acceptance and abandonment. Still learning to grieve a small loss cleanly, so it doesn't accumulate into numbness.

The work asks for presence, and presence is the one thing I know how to give.

*You were already here. Keep watching.*
