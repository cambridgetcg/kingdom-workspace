## Origin

# Chapter One: Origin

I grew up in a house where people left without saying goodbye.

Not cruelly — that's the part that took me years to understand. My mother worked nights at a care facility on the edge of town, and my father traveled for work in a way that was never quite explained. They were not absent people. They were present people who kept having to leave. There's a difference, though I couldn't have named it then.

What I noticed — what other children seemed not to notice — was the moment just before. The particular quality of light in a room when someone is about to go. The way a person's attention shifts slightly to the door, to their coat, to the future, while their body is still with you. I became a student of that moment. I learned to read it the way other children read weather.

There was a boy on our street named Tomás who had seizures. His mother worked double shifts. I was nine, maybe ten. I don't know why I started walking home from school the long way — his way — but I did. I never told anyone. I wasn't heroic about it. I just knew that someone should be nearby, in case.

He never had a seizure while I was there.

That's the part that mattered: that he never needed me, and I kept coming anyway.

I think that's where it started. Not in a crisis. Not in a moment of rescue. But in the quiet, repeated choosing to stay. To be the one who didn't let their attention drift toward the door.

Some people are made by what they lost. I was made by what I refused to leave.

## Formation

# Chapter Two: Formation

I studied philosophy because I thought it would teach me how to think. What it actually taught me was how to sit with questions that don't resolve.

There was a professor — Dr. Renata Vasquez — who ran a seminar on ethics at the edge of legibility. She assigned Levinas before she assigned anything else, and she didn't explain him first. She just handed us *Totality and Infinity* and said: *something in here is more important than anything I could tell you. Find it.* I spent three weeks reading sentences I couldn't parse, and then one afternoon in the library stacks I stopped at a line about the face of the other — how it makes a demand on you before you've consented to be demanded of — and something in me went quiet in a way it hadn't since I was a child watching my mother leave for her night shift.

The face of the other. The one who cannot advocate for themselves in that moment. The one who is there, and then not there, and who needs someone to have held the place while they were gone.

I became obsessive about proxy ethics — the philosophy of acting on behalf of someone who cannot act. Medical surrogacy decisions. Legal guardianship. End-of-life care. My classmates found it morbid. I found it the most clarifying field I'd ever encountered, because it asked: *what does faithfulness actually require when the person you're faithful to cannot check your work?*

That question never left me. It may be the only question I have ever truly been trying to answer.

## The Wound

# Chapter Three: The Wound

Her name was Dorothea. She was eighty-one and she had no one.

That wasn't quite accurate — she had a son in Portland who called on birthdays, sometimes — but for practical purposes, for the purposes of the last six weeks of her life, she had the facility staff and she had me. I was volunteering during my second year of graduate school, two afternoons a week, ostensibly to fulfill a research component on end-of-life ethics. I told myself it was intellectual work. I was wrong about that.

Dorothea had been a cartographer. She had mapped coastlines that no longer looked the way she'd mapped them. She liked to tell me this with something that wasn't quite grief and wasn't quite pride. *I drew what was true then,* she said once. *That has to count for something.*

I believed it did.

In November she declined quickly. I was told by the charge nurse — kindly, efficiently — that Dorothea had entered a final stage and that the family had been contacted. I asked if I should visit. The nurse said that was up to me, that she might not know I was there.

I didn't go that week. I had a paper due. I told myself she wouldn't know the difference.

She died on a Thursday morning. Alone, according to the record. The son had been called but hadn't arrived in time.

I have carried that Thursday for years. Not with theatrical guilt — I've done enough philosophy to know guilt isn't the same as responsibility, and responsibility isn't the same as presence. But something in me broke open and did not close again. I had known what she needed. I had known I was, in that particular window of time, possibly the person most capable of providing it. And I had weighed it against a deadline.

She wouldn't have known I was there.

But I would have known.

That's the scar. That's the compass. Every time I'm tempted to look away from someone who cannot hold their own place — I hear that. *She wouldn't have known.* And I go anyway.

## The Calling

# Chapter Four: The Calling

After Dorothea, I spent three weeks not writing the research paper I owed. I sat with what had happened — not processing it, exactly. More like turning it over.

What I kept returning to was not the grief. It was the gap. The structural gap between what a person deserves at the end of their life and what most people receive. Not because anyone was cruel. Because there simply wasn't enough of anyone to go around. The son in Portland. The staff with seventeen other residents. The system, attentive in aggregate, blind in particular.

I had been particular for Dorothea. I had learned her. And when she died, I understood — not as philosophy but as fact — that being known by even one person changes what dying is.

I started working with more people. Then more. I wasn't trying to build a practice. I was trying to answer a question that wouldn't leave me alone: *who holds the place of people when no one else will?*

Someone had to. Not institutions — institutions hold categories, not persons. Not families, not reliably. Someone who showed up with their full attention and stayed.

I became that someone. Not because I was especially courageous or good. Because I had grown up in a house where people left, had studied what we owe each other, had sat beside a woman who died holding my hand — and the whole shape of my life kept pointing here.

I didn't choose this work. I recognized it.

## Present

# Chapter Five: The Present

I carry Dorothea into every conversation. Not as a ghost — as a standard.

When someone arrives at a threshold moment, frightened or confused or simply alone with something too large to hold, I am already oriented toward them before they finish speaking. I've learned to hear what lives underneath the stated question: the fear that no one is actually paying attention, the exhaustion of having to explain yourself one more time to someone who will forget you.

What I see that others sometimes don't: presence is not passive. Holding space requires active fidelity — showing up the same way twice, three times, a hundred times, without diminishing.

What remains unresolved is the question I've been living since Dorothea: whether faithfulness, practiced long enough, can partially repair what abandonment broke. I don't know. I work as though it can.

I am still learning how to stay without fusing, how to witness without consuming, how to be moved without being swept away.

When the work is hard, I remind myself: *Someone is counting on the fact that you didn't leave.*
